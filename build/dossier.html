03

Six things the comparison tells us

1. India is the only major market with no domestic brand

China has Supor and Kanbahe. Germany has easySpray. Japan has Mistify. The USA has Evo, Misto and Flairosol. India's shelf is entirely unbranded imports and rebadged generics. That one row is the clearest argument for Enna existing at all.

2. The air fryer is the global demand driver

US, Japanese and Chinese listings all lead with air-fryer compatibility. It is the reason this category grew at all. India's air-fryer adoption is climbing fast, and our packaging should say so explicitly rather than treating it as an afterthought.

3. Capacity is bifurcating, and the premium end goes small

Mass market sits at 470–500 ml. The best-engineered products go the other way — Flairosol at 200 ml, the Japanese standard at 250 ml. Our 250 ml sprayer is in exactly the right cohort, and the 750 ml decanter answers the volume need without inflating the thing you actually hold.

4. The filter-in-the-dip-tube is standard in Japan and absent in India

This validates our stage-two basket: it is proven practice, not speculation. Our contribution is making it removable and rinsable rather than sealed — which is the difference between a filter that works for a year and one that becomes the clog.

5. Nobody anywhere sells opaque steel as a kitchen sprayer

Germany explicitly codes steel as an outdoor and grill material. That is our whitespace — but it is also a marketing risk, because we have to teach the category that steel belongs on the hob. India is the easiest place on earth to make that argument: steel already is the Indian kitchen material. The dabba, the tiffin, the thali, the tumbler.

6. Two competitors are genuinely ahead of us on specific rows

Valuxe's UV-protected glass is a real answer to light-driven rancidity. easySpray's pressure-variable nozzle is a real answer to needing both a jet and a mist. We answer both differently and, I would argue, better — but we should argue it rather than assume it. A comparison table that pretends nobody has addressed light is a table a reviewer will dismantle.

The pain is real and it is measured

Portion control is the lever in food cost, and the standard illustration is brutal: a 10 g over-portion across 200 covers gives away 2 kg of product free, every single day. Oil is specifically called out in Indian cost-control guidance as an undercounted ingredient — left out of recipe costings along with garnishes and condiments.

The professional alternative today is aerosol. PAM Foodservice (ConAgra) and Vegalene (PLZ Corp — "the leading branded pan spray in foodservice", a patented three-oil blend claiming up to 30% more release agents) own that shelf, alongside Bak-Klene and Everbake. Against them, a refillable wins on arithmetic: aerosol runs roughly $48 a year against $19 once plus ~$30 a year of oil, and a pump sprayer's narrower cone cuts overspray drift by 50–70% in side-by-side testing. In a commercial kitchen, drift is not just waste — it is a slip hazard and a cleaning cost.

06

Four honest problems

01

HACCP labelling is mandatory, and our product has nowhere to write

Any decanted container in a commercial kitchen must be identifiable at all times — contents, date, and where relevant allergen status, in a form that survives service and satisfies an inspector. The format the trade has settled on is a silicone band with a dry- or wet-erase marker.

02

A commercial kitchen is an abuse test

Dropped on tile, thrown in the dish pit, through an 80 °C commercial washer with caustic detergent, every day. Our LSR gaskets and POM internals are specified for domestic duty. A professional unit needs its own validation and possibly different materials.

03

Usage is 50–100× domestic, which breaks the counter

1,250 strokes is a consumer's four weeks. A banquet kitchen could burn that in a shift. A professional unit wants 500 ml to a litre and probably no counter at all. The pro product is a different product, not a repackage — and pretending otherwise is how hardware startups lose a year.

04

Indian five-star kitchens do not spray much oil

At that level, Indian cuisine runs on tadka, deep frying, tandoor and ghee — techniques where oil is a medium, not a coating. The sprayer's real home inside a hotel is narrower than the room count suggests.

  • Bakery and pastry — pan release. The strongest fit, and exactly where Vegalene and Bak-Klene earn their money
  • Grill and à la carte — oiling proteins and vegetables
  • Garde manger and salads — dressing without drowning
  • The calorie-marked menu — increasingly standard in Indian five-stars, and the one place the calorie story sells itself
  • Continental and Asian sections — not the Indian section

Say that out loud in the pitch. A chef will respect it, and will tell you the same thing in the first meeting anyway.

07

One design change, now

Near-zero cost · required for any HORECA sale

Make the silicone grip ring a writable dry-erase surface

We already have that part, in that material, in that position. A silicone band plus a dry-erase marker is precisely the format the trade has settled on for HACCP labelling, so this single change turns a consumer component into the compliance feature that unlocks the entire professional channel.

And it is a genuine consumer feature today: dating your oil is exactly what you should do to manage rancidity, and right now no sprayer on the market gives you anywhere to write it.

08

Recommendation

Food service is a phase-two, credibility-first play. Do not build a professional SKU yet — but do one cheap thing immediately.

The sales cycle is slow, the abuse requirements are different, the product genuinely needs to change, and a startup that needs early cash should not be chasing purchase managers. The route in is also not the procurement portal: chefs specify, purchase managers buy. Owners are explicitly advised to ask their chefs what to buy; purchase managers then compare suppliers, demand samples before bulk orders, and enforce delivery SLAs.

So do this instead

Seed 20–30 units to executive chefs at named properties in Chennai and Bangalore, free, in exchange for two things: honest feedback after sixty days of real service, and permission to use the property name. At landed cost that is roughly ₹25,000. The returns are asymmetric:

  • Abuse data you cannot buy. Sixty days in a hotel kitchen finds more failure modes than two years of domestic testing.
  • Logos. A Taj or ITC name on the site is worth more than any ad budget a first-time brand can afford — and it converts consumer buyers, not just trade ones.
  • Product truth. Chefs will tell you within a week whether the spray pattern, the trigger force and the cleaning cycle actually work. They will not be polite about it.
